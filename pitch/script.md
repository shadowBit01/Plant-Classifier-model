# VanaLens: talk script (about 7.9 minutes)

Generated by scripts/build_deck.py from the speaker notes of pitch/VanaLens.pptx (15 slides). Every number comes from runs/. Times assume about 140 spoken words per minute.

## 1. Title (0:00 to 0:15)
Hi, we are presenting VanaLens. It names common plants from a phone photo, and it is honest when it is not sure. Everything you will see was measured on photos taken after our models were trained.

## 2. People want plant names; apps give false confidence. (0:15 to 0:43)
Everyone walks past plants they cannot name: a tree on the street, a herb in a market, a weed in a field. Plant apps exist, but they give one confident answer, even for a plant they have never seen. General AI chatbots are worse at naming plants from an open question. And if your local plant is missing, you cannot add it without retraining the model.

## 3. Prior plant ID gives one answer and cannot learn new plants. (0:43 to 1:15)
Plant ID is not new. Leafsnap matched leaf outlines; Indian work trains CNNs on a few dozen clean leaf photos and must retrain for every new species; Pl@ntNet, Lens and Seek are closed and give a ranked answer. BioCLIP 2 and DINOv2, which we build on, are encoders, not full systems. Each cell says only what the paper reports. The last row is the point: VanaLens has all of these in one system.

## 4. VanaLens names 607 plants and says when it is unsure. (1:15 to 1:48)
VanaLens is a plant identifier built on two frozen vision models, so there is no fine-tuning. Three ideas make it different. First, no retraining: a new species is added by dropping in a few photos. Second, honest uncertainty: confidence is calibrated, and when it is not sure it returns a short list or says the plant is not in its catalog. Third, it uses context: more photos of the same plant and the location where the photo was taken.

## 5. Frozen encoders, several scorers, then calibration decides. (1:48 to 2:24)
Here is the pipeline. A photo goes through two frozen encoders: BioCLIP 2, trained on the tree of life, and DINOv2, a general visual model. Scorers read those embeddings: a linear probe, a nearest-neighbour search over reference photos that also gives us evidence pictures, and text prototypes built from written plant traits. We fuse them and calibrate with temperature scaling, then multiply in a location prior. Finally a conformal rule decides: one species, a short 'not sure' list, or 'not in catalog'.

## 6. What is new: the system and the evidence, not a new network. (2:24 to 2:57)
We did not invent a new network, and we cite every part we reuse. What is new is the system and the evidence. One: fused, calibrated scorers with sets, genus backoff, an unknown check, location and several photos, all on frozen models. Two: a new species from five photos, no retraining, 81% correct. Three: every answer shows its evidence. Four: tested on Indian plants, on photos taken after the encoders were trained, against baselines on the same photos.

## 7. All test photos postdate the encoders' training data. (2:57 to 3:27)
We built our own dataset, vl600, from iNaturalist research-grade observations. It has 607 species: 400 most observed in India, 200 most observed worldwide, and a short list of common cultivated plants. Together they cover 71.6% of all research-grade plant observations in India. The 12,831 test photos were all taken after July 2025, after BioCLIP 2's training data was collected, which reduces overlap with what the encoders saw in training.

## 8. VanaLens beats BioCLIP 2 on 12,831 unseen photos. (3:27 to 4:06)
On 12,831 test photos, BioCLIP 2 zero-shot gets 87.3% when we give it our 607 names, and 61.6% open-ended. A linear probe on our embeddings gets 93.4%. VanaLens with one photo gets 94.4%, and with up to three photos of the same plant 96.0%. Error bars are 95% bootstrap intervals; the BioCLIP 2 runs have no per-photo file, so no interval. We could not run the Pl@ntNet API or a VLM baseline because we had no API keys. On our second test set, inat_india, the picture is the same: 96.4% versus 93.5%.

## 9. Same photos: far ahead of BioCLIP 2, slightly ahead of a strong probe. (4:06 to 4:46)
Every bar uses the same 12,831 test photos and the same frozen features, nothing tuned on test. Against BioCLIP 2 zero-shot with our 607 names we are +6.8 points ahead. Against the best baseline, a linear probe on both encoders, one photo gives only +0.6 points and the intervals overlap, so we do not claim a big win there; three photos and location give +2.6 points. The real gap is what the baselines cannot do: sets, genus backoff, unknown check, new species, evidence. Bars marked not measured yet come from the baselines run.

## 10. Up to three photos push top-1 accuracy past 95%. (4:46 to 5:21)
One photo gives 94.0%. Adding the location prior lifts it to 94.4%. Letting the user add up to three photos of the same plant lifts it to 96.0%, with the whole confidence interval above 95%. We also tried letting the model abstain when unsure: on vl600 it answers 100.00% of photos at 94.4%, so abstaining barely changes anything here. The safer fallback is the taxonomy: if it backs off to genus or family when unsure, it is right 97.1% of the time.

## 11. Calibrated confidence matches how often it is right. (5:21 to 5:58)
Raw fused scores are overconfident: calibration error is 0.6%. One temperature parameter, fitted on the calibration split, brings it to 0.6%; the final ensemble sits at 0.9%. So when VanaLens says 90%, it is right about 90% of the time. On top we use split conformal prediction with a 90% target: the true species was in the set 94.0% of the time. Because top-1 is already above 90%, the set is almost always a single species, so the short 'not sure' list rarely appears on this data.

## 12. Add a new species with 5 photos and no retraining. (5:58 to 6:33)
To test plants it has never seen, we hid 61 species from the catalog. VanaLens flagged 38% of those photos as 'not in catalog', but also wrongly flagged 6% of known photos, so this check is the weakest part. Then we added the hidden species back with only a few photos each, no retraining. With 5 photos per species, they are named correctly 81% of the time, with 10 photos 87%, and accuracy on the old species stays at 94.0%.

## 13. The app shows the answer, the evidence, and its doubt. (6:33 to 7:01)
This is the VanaLens app, running locally. You upload one to three photos and, optionally, the location. The result card shows the species, a calibrated confidence, the 90% set, the written traits that matched, and the nearest reference photos from the gallery as evidence. A second tab adds a new species from a few photos. On CPU it takes about 4.6 seconds per observation once warm.

## 14. It is not perfect yet, and we know what to fix next. (7:01 to 7:38)
We want to be upfront about the gaps. The unknown-plant check catches only 38% of unseen species and wrongly flags 6% of known photos; next we combine several signals and tune on more held-out species. Conformal sets are almost always one species, so the short list adds little; harder look-alikes or a higher coverage target would make it useful. We could not compare against the Pl@ntNet app or a VLM because we had no API keys. And we use DINOv2, because the newer DINOv3 weights are gated.

## 15. Close (7:38 to 7:56)
To sum up: VanaLens names 607 common plants at 94.4% from one photo and 96.0% from up to three, with calibrated confidence, and adds a new species from a few photos without retraining. Thank you. We are happy to take questions.
